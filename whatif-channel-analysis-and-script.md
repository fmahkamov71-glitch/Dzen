**What the winners share**
1. **Earth or "you" is in danger.** The winners all hit home: the planet, humanity, the viewer. The flops are abstract (the universe shrinking, gold) or have no stakes (the scale of the ocean).
2. **An instant, total change.** "INSTANTLY", "at once", "all", "every", "overnight". It's one big switch with a clear before and after.
3. **A timeline built in.** "Hour by Hour", "How long until…", "1 billion years ago". The viewer knows from the title that the story escalates.
4. **A real event behind the fantasy.** The ISS really will be de-orbited, glaciers really are melting, and 3I/ATLAS was in the news.
5. **Comparisons and swaps.** "Swapped our Moon with other moons" or "every planet" give one idea several payoffs.

## 2. How they write the scripts (from the transcripts)

### The hook (first 20–40 seconds)
It follows the same formula every time:

1. **A visual in present tense, no setup.** "Mountains of ice across the planet explode into water. Entire cities and countries, obliterated." / "This is Earth, 1,000 years in the future. Humans are long gone."
2. **Three questions that promise payoffs.** "Where would all this water go? Which places would be lost, and which would survive? And how long until humanity goes extinct?"
3. **An escalation tease.** "As bad as the initial catastrophe would be, **that's nowhere near the worst of it.**"
4. Sometimes **personal stakes or FOMO.** "And **we'll be alive to see** the International Space Station's complete and total destruction."

### The body
- **Start from what the viewer already pictures.** "When you think of a glacier, you probably have this in mind…" Then: "But in the scientific world… there's a big difference."
- **Escalate in steps.** Each block is worse than the last: mountain glaciers, then ice sheets, then sea ice, which turns out to be the real killer.
- **A timeline.** "Day One", "Within a week", "Within a month", "Within 100 years", "After 10,000 years".
- **Everyday comparisons for every number.** "32 cm. That's a submarine sandwich." / "Taller than a 20-story condo tower." / "A football field traveling the length of America every 10 minutes."
- **The narrator answers the viewer's objections.** "So why doesn't NASA just return it? Well… Then why not just let it stay where it is? Well…"
- **A re-hook at the midpoint.** "It couldn't get worse, could it? **Oh, it could.**"
- **A twist about the real threat.** The killer is the quiet loss of reflective sea ice, not the tsunami.
- **A human moment.** "If you were one of the sole survivors… you'd have access to all kinds of critical information. If you could figure out the Dewey Decimal System."
- **Humor every 60–90 seconds** to break up the doom: polar bears and Coca-Cola ads, "welcome to the island of Arkansas", "Dingos 2.0", "avoid weirdos on power bikes".
- **The sponsor slot sits at 15–25% of runtime** and grows out of the topic itself ("And if we're talking solar panels…"). Then they return with "Now, back to…".

### The ending
- A long-timescale view (millions or billions of years).
- A link back to real life ("current science says…").
- The signature line: **"But that's a story for another WHAT IF."** It also teases the next episode.

### Voice
Second person ("you"), short sentences, fragments ("Absolute calamity." "Gone."), casual words ("doozies", "gunna", "Heck no"). Metric units come first and imperial follows in parentheses. Facts come from NASA, satellites and named studies.

## 3. Topic for the new script

**What If Earth Lost Its Magnetic Field, INSTANTLY? (Hour by Hour)**

Why this one:
- It hits every current winner pattern: Earth in danger, an instant switch, Hour by Hour, and a real hook (the field is weakening, the South Atlantic Anomaly, magnetic north drifting toward Siberia).
- It isn't in the last 50 long-form uploads, so it isn't a repeat.
- It has a strong opening visual (aurora over the whole planet, including the equator) and a planned twist: the expected killer (radiation and losing the atmosphere) isn't the real one.
- It pairs naturally with the channel's past hits on Mars, the Sun and the ISS.

Title alternatives:
- *What If Earth's Magnetic Field Vanished Tomorrow? (Hour by Hour)*
- *You Are Alive to Watch Earth's Magnetic Field Weaken*
- *What If Earth's Shield Turned Off for 24 Hours?*

Thumbnail: half of Earth with a glowing blue magnetic shield that "shatters" like glass on the Sun's side. Big text: **"SHIELD: OFF"**.

### COLD OPEN (0:00–0:40)

`[VISUAL] Night. A desert town near the equator. The sky suddenly ripples green and red, from horizon to horizon. People come out of their houses with their phones.`

It's 2 a.m. in Singapore, and the sky is on fire. Green curtains of light are rolling over the equator, where nobody alive has ever seen an aurora.

`[VISUAL] A plane cockpit. Instruments flicker. The compass needle spins slowly and stops at nothing.`

Every compass on Earth just stopped pointing north. And 4.5 billion years of protection just switched off.

`[MUSIC] A low drone builds.`

So what happens when Earth loses its magnetic field? Would the Sun cook us? Would our atmosphere be blown into space? And how long would humanity last without the shield we never knew we had?

As beautiful as tonight's sky is… it's the last good news you're going to hear.

`[SFX] Bass hit. Title card: WHAT IF.`

---

### PART 1: The shield you never think about (0:40–3:00)

`[VISUAL] A classic school diagram: a bar magnet with field lines and a compass.`

When you think about Earth's magnetic field, you probably picture this. A big bar magnet. A compass. Maybe a school science project with iron filings.

`[VISUAL] Zoom through the crust and mantle into the liquid outer core. Swirling orange currents.`

But the real thing is much stranger, and much more alive. About 2,900 km (1,800 mi) beneath your feet is an ocean of molten iron and nickel the size of Mars. It churns. It rises and sinks. And because Earth spins, it twists into giant spirals.

Moving metal makes electric current, and electric current makes a magnetic field. Scientists call it the **geodynamo**. It's a power plant the size of a planet, and it's been running nonstop for billions of years.

`[VISUAL] Pull out to space. The field forms a teardrop "bubble" around Earth, squashed on the Sun's side with a long tail on the night side.`

And it doesn't just stay inside the planet. It reaches tens of thousands of kilometers into space and forms a bubble called the **magnetosphere**. On the side facing the Sun it reaches out about 60,000 km (37,000 mi). On the night side it stretches into a tail millions of kilometers long.

Why does that matter? Because the Sun is constantly trying to sandblast us.

`[VISUAL] The Sun "exhales" a stream of particles. They hit the bubble and flow around it like water around a boat's bow.`

It's called the **solar wind**: a nonstop stream of charged particles coming off the Sun at around 400 km per second (900,000 mph). That's fast enough to get from New York to London in about 15 seconds.

Right now, nearly all of it is hitting the magnetosphere and flowing around us. Like rain on an umbrella you never knew you were holding.

So what happens if someone closes the umbrella?

---

### PART 2: Second 1 to Hour 1 (3:00–6:00)

`[VISUAL] An "OFF" button. The bubble around Earth flickers and disappears. Particles rush straight at the planet.`

Let's say it happens instantly. At exactly midnight, Greenwich Mean Time, the geodynamo stops making a field. Gone.

**Second 1.** Nobody notices. Seriously. You can't feel a magnetic field. You can't see it. Your phone still works and your coffee is still hot.

`[VISUAL] Earth orbit. A GPS satellite, then a weather satellite, its solar panels flashing.`

But 20,000 km (12,400 mi) up, the machines notice.

Earth has two giant rings of trapped radiation called the **Van Allen belts**. They're doughnuts of high-energy particles, and they're held in place by the magnetic field. Without the field, the doughnuts come apart. Trillions of particles that were going around in circles suddenly fly loose.

`[VISUAL] Glowing doughnuts burst, and particles scatter like sparks.`

At the same time, the solar wind comes straight in and hits the upper atmosphere head-on.

**Minute 10.** Satellites start having a very bad night. Their electronics get hit by particles they were never designed to see. Memory chips flip ones and zeros. Some satellites go into "safe mode", which is basically playing dead.

`[VISUAL] Mission Control. A wall of screens, several turning red. An operator takes off their headset.`

This isn't science fiction. We've seen a small preview. In February 2022, SpaceX launched 49 Starlink satellites right into a moderate solar storm. The storm heated and puffed up the upper atmosphere, increasing drag, and **38 of the 49 fell back to Earth** within days.

That was a moderate storm, while the shield was still on.

**Hour 1.** The night sky lights up.

`[VISUAL] Montage: auroras over Cairo, Mumbai, Rio, Lagos, all at once.`

Normally the magnetic field funnels solar particles toward the poles. That's why the Northern Lights belong to places like Norway and Alaska. Without a field, there's no funnel. Particles slam into the atmosphere everywhere.

We know what that looks like because we've seen it on another planet. NASA's MAVEN orbiter has seen aurora across almost the whole night side of Mars, a planet with no global magnetic field.

So tonight, for the first time in human history, kids in Nairobi, Jakarta and Panama City step outside and see the aurora.

It's breathtaking. It's free. And it's a warning light on the planet's dashboard.

---

### PART 3: Day 1, when navigation stops working (6:00–8:30)

`[VISUAL] A ship's bridge in fog. An old analog compass spins slowly.`

By sunrise, the first thing humans notice isn't radiation. It's confusion.

Every magnetic compass on Earth is useless. Most of us switched to GPS long ago, so that sounds fine. But a lot of the world still quietly depends on magnetic north. Airport runways are literally numbered by their compass heading. Runway 27 points roughly toward 270 degrees, which is west. Ships and aircraft carry magnetic compasses as a backup to GPS. And today the backup is broken.

`[VISUAL] A GPS map on a phone: the location dot jumps 30 meters, 100, 300.`

And GPS? It doesn't need the magnetic field to work. But its signals pass through the **ionosphere**, the electrically charged upper layer of our atmosphere. And right now the ionosphere is in chaos, constantly hit by solar particles. The signals are scattered and delayed. Your blue dot starts wandering around the map.

In normal life that's annoying. For a container ship in the Strait of Malacca, or a plane landing in fog, it's dangerous.

`[VISUAL] Nature: a flock of birds circles over the sea, scattered and lost.`

And it's not just humans who are lost.

A huge number of animals have a built-in magnetic sense. Migratory birds. Sea turtles. Salmon. Whales. Homing pigeons. Even certain bacteria, which grow tiny chains of magnetic crystals like compass needles.

A sea turtle hatches on a beach in Florida, swims across the entire Atlantic, and decades later finds its way back to the beach where it was born. Scientists think a big part of that GPS is magnetic.

Today, that GPS is gone.

`[VISUAL] A young turtle swims in a circle in the open ocean.`

Migrations don't stop overnight. Many animals also use the stars, the Sun and smell. But this year, millions of birds land in the wrong places. Fish come back to the wrong rivers. Whales strand on beaches.

The planet's living navigation system has just lost one of its maps.

### PART 4: Week 1, the Sun takes aim (9:30–12:30)

`[VISUAL] The surface of the Sun. A huge loop of plasma erupts and breaks off into space.`

So far, the Sun has just been breathing on us. Now it's going to sneeze.

Every so often the Sun throws off a **coronal mass ejection**, a CME. That's billions of tons of magnetized plasma hurled into space at up to several thousand kilometers per second. With the shield on, Earth takes these hits and shrugs most of them off.

How badly can it go even with the shield? Let's go back to 1859.

`[VISUAL] Sepia: a 19th-century telegraph office. Sparks fly off the equipment and an operator jumps back.`

On September 1st, a British astronomer named Richard Carrington was sketching sunspots when he saw a brilliant white flash on the surface of the Sun. About 17 hours later, it hit Earth.

Auroras lit up the skies as far south as Cuba and Hawaii. People in the northeastern US woke up thinking it was dawn and started making breakfast. Telegraph lines sparked and shocked their operators. Some telegraph systems kept sending messages with their batteries **disconnected**, running on current that was pouring in from the sky.

That's the **Carrington Event**. In 1859 we had telegraphs. Today we have everything.

`[VISUAL] 1989. Snow-covered Quebec. The city goes dark district by district.`

A smaller version happened in 1989. A solar storm induced currents in Quebec's power grid, and in about 90 seconds, 6 million people lost power. In winter.

Now take those events and remove most of the umbrella.

Without a magnetosphere, Earth still isn't completely naked. Here's a twist most people don't expect: when the solar wind hits the upper atmosphere directly, it creates a weak, artificial magnetic "bubble" of its own. Venus has exactly this kind of **induced magnetosphere**. Venus has no internal magnetic field at all, and it still has an atmosphere 90 times thicker than ours.

So no, the Sun doesn't blow our air away in a week. Physics is a little on our side.

But that weak bubble is much smaller and much leakier than the real thing. So when the first big CME arrives…

`[VISUAL] A cloud of plasma hits Earth. The atmosphere glows. Transformers explode in a chain across a continent at night.`

…it lands much harder than usual. Huge electric currents flow through the ionosphere and induce surges in the long power lines on the ground. Transformers, the giant, expensive, custom-built kind that take a year or more to replace, start to overheat and fail.

It's not just one city in Quebec. It's whole regions.

Can it get worse than a world without compasses and with collapsing power grids?

Oh, it can.

### PART 5: Month 1, the invisible damage (12:30–15:30)

`[VISUAL] Earth's atmosphere in cross-section. A thin blue layer high up is labeled "OZONE".`

There's something happening above our heads that we can't see.

About 15 to 35 km (9 to 22 mi) up, Earth has its own sunscreen: the **ozone layer**. It absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet light, the kind that causes sunburn and skin cancer and damages DNA.

When high-energy particles slam into the upper atmosphere, they create molecules called nitrogen oxides, and nitrogen oxides destroy ozone. Normally this only happens a little, near the poles, where the magnetic field lets particles in.

Now the "poles" are everywhere.

`[VISUAL] A world map. An "ozone hole" spreads out from the poles toward the middle latitudes like a stain.`

Every big solar storm eats into the ozone layer. And there's no recovery time between storms. Within months, UV levels at the surface start creeping up.

`[VISUAL] A sunny beach. Lifeguards put up a sign: "UV INDEX: 14 — EXTREME".`

What does that mean for you? Sunburns faster than ever. A short walk in the noon sun turns into a medical risk. Over years, skin cancers and cataracts rise. Plants and plankton, the base of the ocean food chain, get stressed by UV.

Is there any evidence for this? About 42,000 years ago, Earth's magnetic field almost collapsed. It's called the **Laschamp excursion**. For several centuries the field dropped to a small fraction of its current strength.

`[VISUAL] Neanderthals in a cave mouth looking up at red auroras. Hand stencils in red ochre on the cave wall.`

In 2021, a team of scientists published a study in the journal *Science* linking that period to ozone damage, climate shifts and even a spike in cave art. Their idea was that people moved into caves and used ochre as sunscreen. They half-jokingly called it the **Adams Event**, after *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*, because it happened about 42,000 years ago.

Many researchers think the study goes too far. But it shows that scientists take the question seriously. A weak shield means a harsher Sun.

### PART 6: Year 1, life in a world without a shield (15:30–18:00)

`[VISUAL] Earth from orbit, quieter now. Fewer lit-up cities at night than before.`

A year in, humanity is adapting. As always.

**Space.** Low Earth orbit is a junkyard of dead or crippled satellites. Satellite operators fight a constant battle with radiation and atmospheric drag. Astronauts? Missions get shorter. The radiation dose outside the shield is no joke. Going to Mars suddenly feels a lot less like a vacation.

**Flying.** Airlines change routes. At cruising altitude, around 11 km (36,000 ft), there's much less air above you to stop cosmic rays. Pilots and flight crews, already among the most radiation-exposed workers on Earth, get new dose limits. Polar flights? They're out.

`[VISUAL] A pilot in the cockpit puts on a badge-style dosimeter.`

**On the ground.** Here's the good news, the part the internet always gets wrong: you're not going to get radiation sickness walking down the street.

Even without a magnetic field, you're sitting under about 10 tons of air per square meter. That's the same protection as standing under a 10-meter (33-foot) pool of water. The atmosphere is the real armor. The magnetic field is more like a force field in front of the armor.

So the dose on the ground goes up, especially at high altitudes and during storms, but not enough to cook us.

`[VISUAL] Engineers in hard hats at a substation, installing large gray boxes.`

**The grid.** Countries rebuild their power systems with surge blockers, spare transformers and "space weather" forecasts treated as seriously as hurricanes. Every evening the news shows the solar storm forecast. Big storm tomorrow? Power companies ease off the grid ahead of time.

In other words, humanity survives. Uncomfortable, a bit more sunburned, but alive.

Then what's the problem?

---

### PART 7: 1 million to 1 billion years, the slow theft (18:00–20:30)

`[VISUAL] A side-by-side comparison: blue Earth and red Mars.`

To see the real price, look at our neighbor.

About 4 billion years ago, Mars probably looked a bit more like Earth. It had rivers. Lakes. Maybe an ocean. Thicker air. And Mars had a magnetic field.

`[VISUAL] Ancient Mars with blue water and clouds, slowly turning into a dry red desert.`

Then its core cooled and its geodynamo stopped. The shield disappeared. And the solar wind went to work.

It didn't happen in a day, a year or a thousand years. It happened slowly, molecule by molecule. NASA's MAVEN spacecraft is watching it happen right now: Mars is still losing roughly 100 grams of atmosphere every second, and much more during solar storms.

100 grams. That's a bar of chocolate per second. Doesn't sound like much? Multiply that by a billion years.

Today, Mars has an atmosphere less than 1% as thick as ours. Its water? Some is frozen underground or at the poles. Some is gone forever into space.

`[VISUAL] Earth, sped up: the atmosphere glows faintly at the top while a thin stream of particles trails off into space.`

Earth is bigger and heavier than Mars, so it holds its gas better. Venus shows a planet can keep a thick atmosphere without a field. So we're not about to turn into Mars.

But over hundreds of millions of years, things change. The solar wind slowly strips hydrogen from the upper atmosphere. And hydrogen comes from water. Molecule by molecule, the oceans leak into space.

`[VISUAL] Oceans slowly retreat from the coastlines, millions of years per second.`

This is the real threat. It isn't a tsunami or a fireball. It's a slow, silent theft that no single person would ever notice. Every generation lives on a planet that seems the same as their grandparents'. And yet, step by step, Earth turns into Mars.

---

### PART 8: The twist, is it already happening? (20:30–22:30)

`[VISUAL] A real map of Earth's field strength. A big blue "bruise" spreads over South America and the South Atlantic.`

Okay. Earth's magnetic field isn't going to switch off at midnight. A dynamo the size of a planet doesn't just stop.

But it's not standing still either.

Our magnetic field has weakened by about 9% in the last two centuries. Over the South Atlantic there's a huge weak spot called the **South Atlantic Anomaly**. Satellites passing through it regularly have glitches, and the Hubble Space Telescope doesn't take observations while it's crossing it.

`[VISUAL] An Arctic map. Over the decades, a magnetic north pole marker slides from Canada toward Siberia.`

And magnetic north is on the move. For most of the 20th century it crept along slowly. Then it sped up. In recent decades it has been running from Canada toward Siberia at up to 50 km (30 mi) a year, and only recently has it started to slow down.

Is this the start of a **pole reversal**? It's happened hundreds of times in Earth's history. North becomes south and south becomes north. The last full flip was about 780,000 years ago.

During a reversal the field doesn't disappear completely. It weakens, gets messy, and can have several "north poles" at once. And it doesn't happen in a day. It probably takes thousands of years.

So no, you're not going to wake up tomorrow to aurora over Singapore.

`[VISUAL] Back to the cold open: the same desert town, the same kid with a phone. This time the sky is just starry.`

But the next time you look at a compass, remember: that little needle points at an ocean of molten iron 3,000 km under your feet. An ocean that's been protecting your air, your water and your sky for 4 billion years. And it does it every second, without asking anything in return.

Mars lost its shield. Venus survived without one. And Earth? For now, the dynamo keeps turning.

`[VISUAL] Fly down into the core. The glow of the molten iron. Darkness.`

And if you're wondering what would happen if the core didn't just stop making a field, but stopped **spinning** altogether…

Well, that's a story for another WHAT IF.

`[SFX] End sting. End screen with 2 recommended videos ("What If You Lived on Mars 1 Billion Years Ago?", "What If You Dug to the Earth's Core?").`

## 5. Production notes

- **Voice:** one narrator, calm, a little ironic. Pauses before the twists ("Oh, it can.").
- **Humor:** a light gag every 60–90 seconds (the breakfast at dawn in 1859, the turtle swimming in circles, "a bar of chocolate per second"). Without these, 20 minutes of doom gets heavy.
- **Re-hooks** at roughly 3:00 ("what happens if someone closes the umbrella?"), 12:30 ("Oh, it can"), 15:30 ("Then what's the problem?") and 20:30 ("is it already happening?"). These counter the usual retention drops.
- **Numbers** always come with an everyday comparison: a pool of water, New York to London, a bar of chocolate.
- **Fact check before recording** (these are rounded from public sources: NASA, ESA, NOAA, *Science* 2021): the 9% weakening, Starlink 38 of 49 (Feb 2022), Quebec 1989 (~6M people, about 90 s to collapse), MAVEN's ~100 g/s, Laschamp ~42,000 years ago, the last reversal ~780,000 years ago.
- **Chapters:** Cold open · The shield · Hour 1 · Day 1 · Week 1 · Month 1 · Year 1 · A billion years · Is it already happening?
